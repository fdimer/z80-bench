Click any ringed pin for its fault menu. There are two kinds of fault. A line held high or low is a short on the wire: every pin on that line, on every chip, sees the level and shows it. A pin disconnected from its line (tick the box) is a broken trace or a lifted pin: only that chip is affected. A disconnected input floats high, or can be given a level of its own; a disconnected output no longer drives its line, which then floats high for the pins that listen to it. A disconnected pin ignores a fault on its line. Faults are listed in the Debug pane and stay until cleared there, in the Memory chips panel, or by Master reset.

Clicking the Z80's CLK pulses one clock (right-click it for its fault menu). On the ZX81 the ULA (IC1) makes the clock, selects the ROM and the RAM, reads the keyboard and the tape, and builds the picture: it feeds the Z80 a NOP for every character it takes from the display file, then drives A0'–A8' during the refresh half of that fetch to pick the character's row in the ROM. Two sets of resistors make this possible: the ULA's address pins are on the ROM and RAM side of 1K resistors (A0'–A8', a line of their own apart from the Z80's A0–A8), and its data pins are on the Z80's side of 470 ohm resistors (D0–D7, apart from the ROM's and RAM's D0'–D7'). The Z80's INT pin is wired to A6. The ULA's pinout is from Sinclair's circuit diagram; Ferranti published no data sheet, so what happens inside it is a model of its behaviour, and the moment of each change within a character is approximate. TV/TAPE shows S for sync, B for black and W for white. The ZX80 has no ULA (its video is built from logic chips that are not drawn here), so only the Z80 is shown for it.

Memory chips live pins · click a pin for its fault menu

The machine's memory chips: the ROM (IC2: the 4K 2332 of the ZX80, or the 8K 2364), the two 2114s of the built-in 1K (IC3 and IC4 on the ZX80, IC4a and IC4b on the ZX81), the single 2K 4016 that a Timex Sinclair 1000 has in position IC4 instead (read from a real board) or, with a RAM pack, its 4116 dynamic RAMs, one per data bit in each 16K. A tile's dot lights while its chip is being accessed. Click a tile to see that chip's pins, which follow the bus on every clock. Assumed: the first 2114 holds data bits 0–3, and the RAM pack is wired in the usual way (its chips have no board numbers here). The 8K of the hi-res board is not included.

To simulate a fault, click a pin for its menu. On the ZX81, hold the whole line high or low and every pin on that line is affected, on every chip: the ROM and the built-in RAM share the address lines A0'–A8' with the ULA, their data pins share D0'–D7', and each has its select line from the ULA. Or tick "Disconnected from the line" for a fault on this chip's pin alone. On the ZX80, and on a RAM pack's chips, every pin counts as a line of its own: Disconnected lifts it (an input then floats high), High and Low hold it at that level. An address pin fault makes the chip use the wrong cell; a data pin fault changes that bit on reads and writes; chip select held high (or a fault on RAS or CAS of a dynamic RAM) takes the chip out of circuit; write enable held high loses writes, held low turns every access into a write. A chip with a fault is marked in red, and every fault is listed in the Debug panel, where it can be removed. The memory view and the disassembly show what the CPU reads through the faults.

Program

Standard Zilog syntax, one instruction per line. Labels end with :; comments start with ;. Directives: ORG, EQU, DB, DW, DS, END. Numbers: 1Fh, 0x1F, $1F, %1010, 'A', and $ for the current address. Undocumented instructions (IXH, SLL, OUT (C),0) are accepted. Code starts at the Load at address; an ORG in the source overrides it. Import a file with the button or by dropping it on the editor.

Hex bytes separated by spaces or newlines. Text after ; is ignored. Write 0100: to continue loading at another address. Intel HEX files (lines starting with :) are also accepted and keep their own addresses. Raw binary ROM images (.bin, .rom) import here as hex. Memory

Scroll through all 64 KB. Click a byte to edit it. Blue marks the instruction being executed, amber marks bytes written by the last instruction, underline marks the top of the stack. With Follow PC ticked, each step brings the current instruction back into view; untick it to keep watching another area.

Memory bitmap

Point at the bitmap to read a byte; click to show it in Memory.

Every bit of memory is one pixel. Each byte is a row of 8 pixels (bit 7 on the left) and each strip is one 256-byte page, read top to bottom; each band holds up to 8K. The background colour shows what each part of RAM is being used for, read from BASIC's own system variables, so you can watch the program, the display file and the stack grow and move. In the ROM, the tinted pages are the ones the video logic reads characters from (the I register), where the character set appears as letters. The red mark is the program counter and the blue mark the stack pointer.
